### Motivation
Making a career transition and working in tech can be really stressful and it’s important to arm yourself with tools that will help you learn to deal with that stress in a healthy way. Otherwise, stress can show up as or result in depression, anxiety, physical health issues like chronic back pain, and burnout that keeps you from being able to do anything.

Mental wellness and self-care are essential career skills for career changers developing software skills and adapting to remote technical work. Building awareness of thought patterns, stress responses, and personal limits can make it easier to learn consistently, ask questions, receive feedback, communicate with teammates, and remain resilient through unfamiliar tools and challenges. These practices support sustainable growth without equating productivity with constant availability or overwork.

### Objectives
By the end of the sessions, participants should be able to:

- Recognize how thoughts, emotions, and behaviors influence technical learning and workplace communication.
- Identify anxious thinking patterns that may affect asking for help, contributing in meetings, or sharing work for review.
- Use grounding and self-regulation techniques before responding to stressful messages, feedback, deadlines, or technical problems.
- Apply time-management strategies to structure remote learning, focused development work, meetings, and recovery time.
- Build confidence through realistic self-reflection rather than perfectionism or comparison with more experienced developers.
- Create a personal plan for communicating needs, setting boundaries, and seeking support on professional technical teams.

### Specific Things To Learn
- How the cognitive model applies to debugging, code reviews, interviews, stand-ups, and unfamiliar technical tasks.
- How cognitive distortions such as catastrophizing, mind reading, imposter thinking, and all-or-nothing thinking can affect career development.
- How to challenge thoughts such as “I should already know this” or “One mistake proves I am not ready.”
- Grounding techniques for managing stress during remote meetings, difficult conversations, technical blockers, or intense study sessions.
- How internal “hijackers” can trigger defensiveness, withdrawal, over-explaining, or avoidance when receiving feedback.
- Time-management practices for balancing upskilling, job searching, team responsibilities, and personal wellbeing.
- How to use pauses, clarifying questions, written follow-ups, and support channels to communicate effectively on technical teams.
- The difference between healthy persistence, sustainable effort, avoidance, and burnout.

### Lesson
1. Connect wellness to technical growth: Reflect on a recent learning or work challenge. Record the situation, automatic thought, emotion, behavior, and result using the cognitive model.
1. Identify unhelpful patterns: Review the cognitive distortions material and consider how these patterns might appear during code reviews, pair programming, interviews, team discussions, or remote collaboration.
1. Reframe the challenge: Use Challenging Anxious Thoughts to replace one discouraging thought with a balanced, evidence-based perspective and a practical next step.
1. Practice regulation: Use a grounding exercise before imagining a stressful technical-team situation, such as receiving critical feedback, being blocked on a task, or speaking in a meeting.
1. Plan sustainable remote work: Select time-management strategies that support focused practice, realistic task planning, communication availability, breaks, and recovery.
1. Strengthen team communication: Reflect on how to pause before reacting, ask for clarification, communicate a blocker early, and request feedback without interpreting it as a judgment of ability.
1. Create an action plan: Define one wellness practice, one learning habit, one communication behavior, and one boundary to apply during the next week of technical development.

### Self-Care
There are a lot of ways to take care of yourself, but the biggest one is probably learning to draw boundaries. This means curbing people-pleasing and saying no to protect your wellness. For example, if your workload feels too heavy and your boss gives you yet another assignment, you can say, “I’d love to take that on, but I’m not sure I have the bandwidth for it right now. What does your timeline look like? If you need this right away, what would you like me to deprioritize in order to take this assignment and give it the attention it needs?” One book that can be really helpful is [Set Boundaries, Find Peace by Nedra Tawwab](https://www.nedratawwab.com/books/set-boundaries-find-peace). 

Taking the time to just breathe, feel gratitude, talk to friends, and enjoy little things in life is also important. Here are some ideas shared by past participants: 

- Go on a walk
- Do yoga
- Watch a movie
- Try a new recipe
- Thoughtfully clean your bedroom
- Breathe in a scent you really love
- Meet a friend at a coffee shop, cafe, or the park
- Do something creative like drawing, painting, crocheting, or working on a puzzle
- Go to the spa or get a massage
- Listen to happy, calming music
- Do your nails
- Read a book
- Do some stretches
- Drink a calming drink
- Cry
- Take a nap
- Write in your journal
- Meditate/do breathing visualizations of letting something go or being as strong as a mountain

Making a Wellness Recovery Action Plan, or WRAP, can help you outline stressors and make a plan for dealing with stress. Feel free to make a copy of [our WRAP template](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1cj0ASEkUJUwJIdTbLXnwaJFKtBEbrV1kcIpq5oL5bGE/edit?usp=sharing) and adjust it for you so you can refer to it when you’re feeling too stressed to even think of what to do. It’s good to share your WRAP with people who care about you so they can also be helpful when needed.  

Studies have shown that venting, or “letting out” anger and raising your heart rate does not regulate it. Instead, lower your heart rate with deep breaths, meditation, and yoga. Anger can also simmer and grow when you avoid giving feedback. Here’s [our feedback presentation](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1kaTYV_PoKpMukLSAev4MmjfFR02nTUCfjwD6rRptbk0/edit?usp=sharing) to give you some reminders about how you can share your feedback before it piles up. 

[A Stanford neurobiologist studied methods of dealing with stress and anxiety](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ntfcfJ28eiU), and here’s what was recommended: 

- Do the physiological sigh a few times—two breaths in via the nose followed by a longer, more vigorous exhale.
- Super-oxygenation—breathe really fast 25-30 times followed by a big exhale and hold your breath for 15 seconds and repeat a few times, then a big inhale until you feel the impulse to breathe.
- Take cold showers (though some research has shown this works more for men than for women).
- Box breathing: breathing in for three seconds, pausing for three seconds, breathing out for three seconds, pausing for three seconds, and so on, gradually lengthening the time.
- Relax the mind while your body is activated by dilating your gaze (look at the bigger room) to widen your tunnel vision stress response to raise your stress response for medium-length stress.
- Long-term stress: regular exercise, good diet, and sleep. Social connection to people, pets, and other things that delight us (Serotonin is released when we see someone we recognize or we’re delighted).
- Recognize things you’re grateful for.
- Take Ashwagandha (lowers chronic/total cortisol) and/or L-Theanine 100-200 mg 30-60 minutes before sleep (reduces ruminating, decreases task completion anxiety, notably reduces effects of stress).
- Also, from Michelle: remember to prioritize the recovery (schedule time to recharge after the crunch time is over). 

Burnout is very common in tech. Learn more about its causes and how to deal with it in [our Burnout presentation](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1-fbFE1qCSiNk6kyBopfQnEDlbFYtyJnMQTlwv3lJMs0/edit?usp=sharing) or in the book [Burnout: The Secret to Unlocking the Stress Cycle by Emily Nagoski and Amelia Nagoski](https://www.burnoutbook.net/). One important thing to remember is that you don’t have to immediately quit your job to deal with burnout, and it’s more difficult to escape burnout if you also have to deal with the constant rejections that come with the job search. Instead, we recommend you are honest with your boss about it so the two of you can come up with a plan to spread out your workload and check in regularly about how you’re doing. If you reach the point of exhaustion, you can work out sick leave with your boss so you can come back to your job after some recovery.

### Meditation
Meditation and focusing on slowing your breath and letting out sighs can help your body calm down and release stress by activating your vagus nerve. Some guided meditations focus solely on breathing and relaxing your body, while others are just music that help you be present, and many have guided visualizations, like letting something go or being as strong as a mountain to ground yourself.

### Therapy
Therapy can help you learn more about yourself and gain tools for dealing with the ups and downs of life. Here are some resources: 

- [If-me](https://www.if-me.org/resources) (this is curated by an open-source community!)
- [Two Chairs](https://www.twochairs.com/) (Bay Area, Los Angeles, Seattle, Miami)
- [Open Path](https://openpathcollective.org/) (California)
- [Sliding scale therapists in San Francisco](https://web.archive.org/web/20191117194704/https://www.wellsanfrancisco.com/about/sliding-scale-therapy/)
- [University of San Francisco sliding-scale therapy list](https://myusf.usfca.edu/dean-of-students/ocrs/sliding)
- [Liberation Institute](https://www.liberationinstitute.org/) (affordable therapy in California and Oregon)

Finding a therapist who works well for you can be tricky. Many therapists will offer short consultations before you commit to working with them, and their websites often list what kinds of issues they have experience with or specialize in. Here are some questions to ask when assessing a therapist: 

- Do you feel like they’re trustworthy, empathetic, neutral, and able to build a good personal rapport with you?
- Do they have experience with the issues you’d like help with?
- Can they clearly outline the issues you’ve shared, point out what they think they can work on with you, and recommend a minimum number of weeks without giving guarantees?
- Do they have a graduate degree in Psychology, Social Work, Marriage and Family Therapy, Behavioral Science, or Counseling (LPCC?) Is their license up-to-date without issues?
- How well do their values align with yours? 
- Are they accepting new clients? 
- Do they seek regular peer consultation? 
- Have they done their own therapy?
- Do they adhere to ethical standards?
- Which methods are you interested in and which methods do they use? 
- Can they accept feedback or admit to making mistakes?
- Do they encourage you to learn how to deal with issues on your own rather than giving you their answers?

### Common Mistakes & Misconceptions
- Assuming career changers must hide uncertainty to appear qualified on technical teams.
- Treating difficulty with a new framework, codebase, or workflow as evidence of personal inability.
- Interpreting code review comments as criticism of character rather than information that improves the work.
- Waiting until a task is nearly overdue before communicating a blocker or asking for help.
- Believing remote work requires constant availability, immediate replies, or working beyond sustainable hours.
- Using time-management techniques to fit in more work without protecting breaks and recovery.
- Avoiding meetings, collaboration, or feedback in the name of self-care when gradual participation would build confidence.
- Expecting grounding or cognitive reframing to remove all stress instead of helping create a more intentional response.
- Comparing a developing skill set with someone else’s years of professional experience.
- Assuming self-care replaces mentorship, manager communication, employee resources, or professional mental-health support.
