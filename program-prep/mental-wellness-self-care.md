# Mental Wellness & Self Care

### Projected Time
3–4 independent sessions of 30–45 minutes each, with optional practice between sessions.

### Motivation
Mental wellness and self-care are essential career skills for career changers developing software skills and adapting to remote technical work. Building awareness of thought patterns, stress responses, and personal limits can make it easier to learn consistently, ask questions, receive feedback, communicate with teammates, and remain resilient through unfamiliar tools and challenges. These practices support sustainable growth without equating productivity with constant availability or overwork.

### Objectives
By the end of the sessions, participants should be able to:

- Recognize how thoughts, emotions, and behaviors influence technical learning and workplace communication.
- Identify anxious thinking patterns that may affect asking for help, contributing in meetings, or sharing work for review.
- Use grounding and self-regulation techniques before responding to stressful messages, feedback, deadlines, or technical problems.
- Apply time-management strategies to structure remote learning, focused development work, meetings, and recovery time.
- Build confidence through realistic self-reflection rather than perfectionism or comparison with more experienced developers.
- Create a personal plan for communicating needs, setting boundaries, and seeking support on professional technical teams.

### Specific Things To Learn
- How the cognitive model applies to debugging, code reviews, interviews, stand-ups, and unfamiliar technical tasks.
- How cognitive distortions such as catastrophizing, mind reading, imposter thinking, and all-or-nothing thinking can affect career development.
- How to challenge thoughts such as “I should already know this” or “One mistake proves I am not ready.”
- Grounding techniques for managing stress during remote meetings, difficult conversations, technical blockers, or intense study sessions.
- How internal “hijackers” can trigger defensiveness, withdrawal, over-explaining, or avoidance when receiving feedback.
- Time-management practices for balancing upskilling, job searching, team responsibilities, and personal wellbeing.
- How to use pauses, clarifying questions, written follow-ups, and support channels to communicate effectively on technical teams.
- The difference between healthy persistence, sustainable effort, avoidance, and burnout.

### Lesson
1. Connect wellness to technical growth: Reflect on a recent learning or work challenge. Record the situation, automatic thought, emotion, behavior, and result using the cognitive model.
1. Identify unhelpful patterns: Review the cognitive distortions material and consider how these patterns might appear during code reviews, pair programming, interviews, team discussions, or remote collaboration.
1. Reframe the challenge: Use Challenging Anxious Thoughts to replace one discouraging thought with a balanced, evidence-based perspective and a practical next step.
1. Practice regulation: Use a grounding exercise before imagining a stressful technical-team situation, such as receiving critical feedback, being blocked on a task, or speaking in a meeting.
1. Plan sustainable remote work: Select time-management strategies that support focused practice, realistic task planning, communication availability, breaks, and recovery.
1. Strengthen team communication: Reflect on how to pause before reacting, ask for clarification, communicate a blocker early, and request feedback without interpreting it as a judgment of ability.
1. Create an action plan: Define one wellness practice, one learning habit, one communication behavior, and one boundary to apply during the next week of technical development.

### Common Mistakes & Misconceptions
- Assuming career changers must hide uncertainty to appear qualified on technical teams.
- Treating difficulty with a new framework, codebase, or workflow as evidence of personal inability.
- Interpreting code review comments as criticism of character rather than information that improves the work.
- Waiting until a task is nearly overdue before communicating a blocker or asking for help.
- Believing remote work requires constant availability, immediate replies, or working beyond sustainable hours.
- Using time-management techniques to fit in more work without protecting breaks and recovery.
- Avoiding meetings, collaboration, or feedback in the name of self-care when gradual participation would build confidence.
- Expecting grounding or cognitive reframing to remove all stress instead of helping create a more intentional response.
- Comparing a developing skill set with someone else’s years of professional experience.
- Assuming self-care replaces mentorship, manager communication, employee resources, or professional mental-health support.
